From Short Story to Fully-Voiced Game in a Day

The pipeline I used to turn 10,000 words of prose into a browser sound novel — and how I made the prose worth voicing in the first place

On a Saturday I finished a short story. By Sunday it was a fully-voiced, illustrated sound novel you could play in a browser tab. A day after that it spoke seven languages.

Play it: The Hundred Misdeeds — free, no install, about thirty minutes.

I want to walk through the whole pipeline, because the interesting part isn't any single tool. It's the order of operations, and one decision made at the start that does most of the work: finish the prose completely before writing a line of engine code. Do it the other way and you're holding two kinds of uncertainty at once — is the story good, and does the code work — and neither converges.

So: the story first.

Part 1: Making the prose good, quickly

A voiced game amplifies whatever you feed it. Thirty minutes of mediocre writing read aloud by a good voice is thirty minutes of mediocre writing that now takes longer to skip. The prose has to carry it.

Mine for an unsaturated premise

Before writing anything I ran a frequency analysis over a personal library of 29,308 book records — not the blurbs, the titles, because in the Japanese web-novel market titles literally describe the plot beats, which makes them a machine-readable map of what's already crowded.

The result was blunt. "Villainess": 330. "Demon lord": 347. Saturated. But "devotion/self-sacrifice": 24. "Letters": 24. "Metafiction": 31. Those are thin seams.

So the design brief wrote itself: a demon-lord story — the crowded shelf, because that's where readers already are — carrying the rare cargo. Devotion. A found document. A narrator with an agenda.

If you take one thing from this article, take this one. It costs an afternoon and it aims everything downstream.

Decide five things yourself; delegate the rest

I use AI heavily in this project, and it is very good at consistency and very bad at knowing what should hurt. So five decisions stay with me, one line each:

  1. The feeling left behind. Not the plot — the residue. Mine: make it impossible to hate this villain properly.
  2. The protagonist's strength, and its price. Same sentence, always. Mine: he can hate hard enough to survive anything — which is the only reason he survived.
  3. The pressure the world applies. Mine: people cannot unite without an enemy.
  4. The central asymmetry. Two equals produce no story. Mine: the one person who knows the truth about her is the one who killed her.
  5. The landing, and the size. Mine: one short story; ends on the hundredth entry.

Everything else — supporting cast, place names, prose drafting, continuity — is delegable. But only because those five are fixed. Hand a model a vague brief and you get something coherent that no one remembers.

Track foreshadowing in a ledger, then verify it mechanically

The story's whole structure is a verification: a hero audits a ledger of one hundred crimes, and one by one they turn inside out. That only lands if every planted detail pays off.

So I kept my own ledger — items A through Q, each with where it's planted, where it detonates, and what it recontextualizes. Then I checked it the way you'd check code: a script that asserts every payoff keyword appears in the final text, downstream of its setup. It's a crude test. It caught two things I'd dropped.

The result, judged the only way that counts

Here's a passage. The hero is verifying misdeed number twenty-two — three hundred children, stolen. He's found the ledger's marginal notes:

"Mira, six. Hives from tree nuts."
"Toto, nine. Cries at night. Do not put out the lamp."
"Sena, five. Deaf in the left ear. Speak from her right."

I held the page, and for a while I could not move.

Why would a record of crimes note nighttime crying? Why would a book of abductions log an allergy? This is not the record of a crime. It is — a handover. Written by someone who cares for children, for whoever will care for them next.

That's the engine of the whole story in nine lines: the same document, read twice, meaning opposite things. Readers report crying around the ten-minute mark, which is roughly where that passage sits.

The prose was the slow part — several days. Everything after it took about a day.

Part 2: The pipeline, stage by stage

Script is data, engine is code

The converter turns prose into a flat list of beats. Dialogue in brackets becomes a line with a speaker; scene markers become background and music changes:

{ bg: 'study' },
{ bgm: 'night' },
{ n: 'Mia', t: '"Lord Ash. Your tea is ready."', v: 'k-799491426' },

Two rules make this hold up:

Don't trust automatic speaker attribution. It's right most of the time, and the times it's wrong the wrong voice comes out of the wrong character, which a player notices instantly. Unresolved lines get flagged and I fill them in by hand.

Split long lines at build time. Anything past ~110 characters gets broken at sentence boundaries. A message window that overflows is the most common way an otherwise finished game looks unfinished.

The engine is one rule

const b = beats[i++];
if (b.bg  !== undefined) { setBG(b.bg);   continue; }  // never blocks
if (b.bgm !== undefined) { setBGM(b.bgm); continue; }
if (b.t   !== undefined) { say(b);        return;   }  // waits for the reader

Commands that change the screen continue immediately. Commands that need a human return and wait. Keep that consistent and the engine stays boring — which is what you want, because it means you can add story without touching it. Save, skip, backlog, and chapter select are the only features that actually matter for a thirty-minute piece; everything else is decoration.

Voice only the leads

This is the highest-leverage cost decision in the whole project.

Full voice for every character means half a gigabyte and a bill to match. Voicing only the two leads costs a fraction — and reads better, because when only two characters speak aloud, the game is telling you whose story this is. Supporting characters in plain text don't feel unfinished; they feel like background.

Files are named by a hash of speaker|text, so there's no manifest to maintain:

function keyOf(t){ let h=0; for (let i=0;i<t.length;i++) h=(h*31+t.charCodeAt(i))|0; return 'k'+h; }

Elegant, with one sharp edge: edit one character of dialogue and that line goes silent forever. The engine requests a file that no longer exists, the browser 404s, playback fails quietly, the game continues. Nothing turns red. On an earlier project I shipped 161 silently muted lines this way.

The fix is bookkeeping, not cleverness — after every revision, recompute every expected filename from the script and diff it against disk in both directions:

referenced=237  on disk=237  missing=0  orphaned=0

Missing files mean silence. Orphans mean something changed and you didn't notice.

Art: lock the seed, share the prefix

Every character is generated from a shared style prefix with a fixed seed, changing only the expression tokens. That's what keeps a cast looking like it belongs to one production rather than five different artists. Cutouts use rembg with the anime-tuned model, which is markedly better than the general one on line art.

The one trap worth naming: adding "flat color, anime style" to the prompt produces a thin-lined flat look that will not match anything else you generate. Leave it out.

Deploy with no build step

Static HTML on GitHub Pages. No bundler, no CI, no build. Push and it's live in under a minute.

This sounds like a minor infrastructure preference. It isn't — it's the reason the whole thing took a day. The moment "deploy" means "build, wait, check," your iteration loop lengthens and you stop making small improvements. One empty .nojekyll file is the entire configuration.

Part 3: Seven languages, almost for free

Localization is usually where a project like this stops. It didn't, because of one build-time change: every dialogue beat got a stable index.

After that, a language is just a JSON array aligned to those indices:

function tr(b){
  if (!L) return { t: b.t, n: b.n };
  return { t: L.t[b.ix] ?? b.t, n: L.names[b.n] ?? b.n };
}

Japanese, English, Chinese, Korean, Spanish, French, German. English voice acting was generated the same way as Japanese, from the same indices, using voices chosen per language rather than reused across both — a voice that sounds warm in one language often doesn't in another.

Then a feature I didn't plan fell out of it nearly free: since every line can be fetched in any language simultaneously, the message window can show two at once. Japanese on top, English underneath. It turns a thirty-minute story into a reading exercise, and it's now the mode I use most.

The logic was trivial; the layout wasn't. Two text blocks in one window fight each other, so the second sits under a thin divider at 85% size and reduced contrast, the window's height cap grows in dual mode, and line-height tightens per viewport. I verified it by asserting in a headless browser that the second block's top edge never crosses the first block's bottom, at desktop, portrait, and landscape sizes.

The three checks I'd keep on any project like this

Everything above is specific. These three generalize:

  1. Inventory your generated assets in both directions. Missing means broken; orphaned means you changed something and didn't notice. Run it after every revision, not before every release.
  2. Verify synthesized audio by listening to it — mechanically. Transcribe every generated clip back and compare it to the source line. A wrong pronunciation, a truncated clip, a hallucinated sentence: none of these throw errors, and all of them are obvious to a transcriber.
  3. Verify the deployed artifact, not your working copy. I once shipped a localization where the engine was deployed and the rebuilt script wasn't, and the fallback I'd written to be safe rendered the whole game in the original language — perfectly, silently, in all seven. My local test passed because the correct file was sitting on my disk. Now the checks fetch the live URL.

That third one is the one I'd tattoo somewhere. A graceful fallback is a decision to convert a loud failure into a quiet one. Sometimes that's right for users. It is never free.
